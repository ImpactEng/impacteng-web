DNS Record Builders

Free, in your browser

Write the email and website security records

These are for whoever looks after your domain or website, so send them this page if that is not you. Five builders for the text records that protect your email and website. Pick one below, fill in a few boxes, and copy the result to wherever your domain's DNS is managed.

One domain, such as yourbusiness.com.au. It stays in this page. The security.txt builder does not need it.

DMARC · SPF · MTA-STS · security.txt · CAA

Your DNS host may want the name without your domain on the end (so _dmarc, not _dmarc.yourbusiness.com.au). If a record is rejected, try that.

DMARC

Step by step

A DMARC plan in four stages

DMARC is the record that tells mail servers what to do with email faking your address. This gives you four versions of it, from "just report to me" to "throw fakes away", so you can tighten it one stage at a time.

Do not use it when: you do not yet know which services send email as you. Stage 1 only asks for reports, so it should not change how your email is delivered; stages 2 to 4 can send your real invoices to junk, or bounce them, if a sender is missing, and they reach your subdomains too.

Standard: RFC 7489. It has since been replaced by RFC 9989, which no longer has the pct setting, so receivers differ: some apply it, and reports show the mail they leave out as sampled_out, and some ignore it. Stage 3 leaves it out, which means 100%.

One email address that you can read. Reports are daily XML files from mail services. If it is at another domain, that domain must agree to receive them (RFC 7489 section 7.1).

SPF

Who may send

An SPF record

SPF is the list of servers allowed to send your email. Tick the services that send mail as you and I write the single record.

Do not use it when: you already have an SPF record that works and you only want to add one service: a domain may have only one, so edit the existing one.

Standard: RFC 7208. A record may cause at most 10 lookups (section 4.6.4).

Services that send email as you

Services marked "get the line from them" are ones where I could not read the line on the service's own page. Get it from them and put it in the extra box below.

Host names only, separated by spaces, from a service that gave you an include line.

Optional. IPv4 addresses, separated by spaces. A block such as 203.0.113.0/24 is fine.

Soft is the safer start. I will not write +all, which lets anyone send as you.

MTA-STS

Encrypted mail

MTA-STS and TLS reports

MTA-STS makes other mail servers use an encrypted connection when they send to you. You publish two records and one small file on your website.

Do not use it when: your mail provider does not let you serve a file on mta-sts.yourdomain, or you cannot keep a certificate for that name running. A broken policy in enforce mode can stop mail arriving.

Standard: RFC 8461 (MTA-STS) and RFC 8460 (TLS reports).

The host names in your MX records, separated by spaces. A leading * works, such as *.example.net.

Start with testing.

The standard allows up to a year.

One email address, or an https address.

security.txt

For researchers

A security.txt file

security.txt is a small text file that tells a stranger who finds a weakness on your website how to tell you.

Do not use it when: nobody will read that mailbox. A file that points to a dead address is worse than none.

Standard: RFC 9116. It goes at /.well-known/security.txt.

An email address or an https address. Separate several with spaces.

A date less than a year ahead, as year-month-day.

An https address where your public key can be fetched. Not the key itself.

Short codes separated by commas.

An https address for how you handle reports.

Where this file lives, over https.

CAA

Who may issue certificates

CAA records

CAA records say which companies may issue a security certificate for your website address. Anyone else asking is meant to be refused.

Do not use it when: you have not checked who issued the certificate on every name you serve, including www and other subdomains. Leave one out and that certificate cannot renew. Nothing fails until it expires.

Your browser shows who issued a certificate, in the certificate details. If yours is not listed below, do not use this builder.

A subdomain with no CAA records of its own uses its parent domain's records, so check your subdomains too. A name that is a CNAME uses its target's CAA records if the target has any. Some hosts, such as Cloudflare under Universal SSL, add CAA records of their own, so dig can show more than you set. That is not an error.

Standard: RFC 8659.

Providers allowed to issue

Most businesses do not have wildcard certificates.

An email address or an https address.

Limits

What this does not do
